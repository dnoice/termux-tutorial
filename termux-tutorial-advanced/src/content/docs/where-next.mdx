its screen, and a binary you compiled yourself for an architecture you probably

And none of this course was simulated. There was no practice terminal on any

handed you. That is the part worth keeping.

## What you can actually do now

Not a list of lesson titles — a list of things you could sit down and do right
now, on a phone, without looking the first step up.

- **Install Termux without walking into the two traps.** From F-Droid or GitHub,
  never the frozen Google Play build, and both halves of a plugin from the
  **same source** — Android's signature-level permission means a mismatched pair
  doesn't error, it just goes silent forever.
- **Drive a shell from a keyboard that has no shell keys on it.** **Volume
  Down + any letter** is Ctrl + that letter, which is the one that saves you.
  **Volume Up** is a short fixed list — `E` for Esc, `T` for Tab, `W`/`A`/`S`/`D`
  for the arrows, `L` for `|`, `H` for `~`, `U` for `_`, and `Q` or `K` to toggle
  the extra-keys row. It is not a general Alt key, and it does not toggle the
  soft keyboard. You also know how to build your own extra-keys row when the
  defaults aren't the keys you keep reaching for.
- **Move files across the Android boundary in the right direction.**
  `termux-setup-storage`, `~/storage/shared` for anything another app has to
  see — and code that stays in `~`, because shared storage has no exec bit and
  no symlinks.
- **Manage software.** `pkg update` before anything, `pkg search` when you don't
  know the name, `pkg list-installed` saved somewhere you can find it after a
  reinstall.
- **Reach the device itself.** Battery, network, sensors, location, camera and
  microphone as commands that print JSON, `jq` to pull one value out, and
  notifications, dialogs, clipboard and speech to answer back.
- **Write a script and let it run without you.** A shebang, `chmod +x`, a `~/bin`
  on your `PATH`, arguments and exit codes — then `cron` or
  `termux-job-scheduler`, plus a straight answer for the overnight silence:
  Doze, App Standby, and the phantom process killer are Android's opinion, not
  your bug.
- **Serve something, then close the door.** A directory over HTTP on the LAN, an
  outbound tunnel when your carrier's NAT rules out port forwarding, and the
  discipline that goes with it — serve a directory built for serving, never `~`,
  never `~/storage/shared`, and assume a public URL is public the second it
  prints.
- **Run a real distribution in user space.** `proot-distro install`, a Debian
  rootfs with `apt`, real paths and real permissions, and an accurate mental
  model of what ptrace-based syscall interception can fake and what it cannot.
- **Put a desktop on the screen.** A display server and a session brought up as
  separate layers, so a black screen is a question with about three answers
  instead of one shrug — and the same desktop driven from inside the container
  once the two halves each work alone.
- **Hand work to the GPU, and know whether it took.** The `x11-repo` packages,
  the driver environment variables, and — the part that matters — a way to check
  which renderer you actually got instead of assuming the flag did something.
- **Build what nobody packaged.** A toolchain, a `./configure` that has never
  heard of Android, an aarch64 binary at the end of it, and "it isn't in the
  repo" demoted from a dead end to an afternoon.

That's not a terminal emulator with tricks. That's a computer you administer
from a touchscreen.

## What Termux is not good for

This matters more than the list above, because knowing where the wall is saves
you the week you'd otherwise spend walking into it. None of these are things you
did wrong.

| You want to… | Why this is the wrong machine |
| :----------- | :---------------------------- |
| Run Docker, LXC, or anything with `systemd` | All of them need real namespaces, cgroups and PID 1. PRoot has none of those, because it has no root — it rewrites syscalls in user space. This is not a missing feature; it's the boundary the whole approach is built on. |
| Load a kernel module, use `iptables`, mount a filesystem | The kernel is Android's, and you are not its administrator. Unrooted means unrooted. |
| Compile something large | PRoot's per-syscall overhead plus thermal throttling makes a phone the slowest reasonable computer in your house for a build. One package, fine. A kernel or a browser, no. |
| Run x86 software | Your phone is almost certainly aarch64. Translation layers exist and are outside this series; they are slow, and "slow" here means slow on top of PRoot's slow. |
| Host anything with an uptime story | Android will kill your process, doze your network, and reboot for an update while you sleep. A tunnel to your pocket is a demo, a debugging aid, a way to show someone a thing. It is not production. |
| Hammer the storage | Phone flash is soldered in and has a finite write budget. A database doing constant writes is spending hardware you cannot replace. |

:::caution[PRoot is not root, and `sudo` inside the container is theatre]
Inside Debian you appear to be `root`, and that appearance is convincing enough
that people build on it for a week before it bites. It is a lie PRoot tells your
processes so that `apt` will run — the *kernel* still sees the same unprivileged
Android UID it always did.

Anything that needs privilege from the kernel rather than from libc fails, and
often fails in a way that reads like a broken package. It isn't. Everything in
the table above is that same fact, wearing different clothes.
:::

:::note[GPU acceleration is the one that may simply not work on your phone]
It was true in that lesson and it's still true now: the driver path depends on
your GPU vendor, your Android version and your device's driver blobs, and there
are phones where no combination of environment variables produces hardware
rendering. If yours is one of them, you did not misconfigure it.

Software rendering still gives you a working desktop. It gives you a desktop
that is slow, hot and correct — which for most of what you'd actually run is the
right trade anyway.
:::

## If you're done for now, take the disk back

A rootfs you're not using is 1.5–3 GB of your phone doing nothing. This is
reversible, which was always the point.

```bash
proot-distro list
```

That prints what's installed alongside what's available. To see what it's
actually costing you:

```bash
du -sh $PREFIX/var/lib/proot-distro/installed-rootfs/
```

Give that one a moment — it walks every file in the rootfs, and there are a lot
of them. If it seems to hang, it hasn't; **Volume Down + C** stops it if you'd
rather not wait.

Then, when you mean it:

```bash
proot-distro remove debian
```

:::caution[That deletes everything inside the container]
`remove` does not archive, prompt twice, or keep a copy. Files in the
container's `/home` and `/root` go with it — anything you want to keep should
already be on the Termux side, in `~`, or backed up to `~/storage/shared`. If
you bind-mounted a Termux directory in, that directory is fine; it was never
inside the rootfs to begin with.
:::

Reinstalling later is one command and a download. Nothing you learned expires.

## Where the ecosystem actually lives

Termux is a volunteer project with a large surface area, and this course is a
path through it, not a map of it. Here's the rest of the map.

| Where | What's there |
| :---- | :----------- |
| [wiki.termux.com](https://wiki.termux.com) | The reference. Every `termux-*` command, the package repos, the plugin apps, and the Community page — which is where you should get chat-room links, because rooms move and tutorials go stale. Including this one. |
| [github.com/termux](https://github.com/termux) | All of it. The app, the packages, the plugins, the issue trackers where device-specific weirdness actually gets diagnosed. |
| [termux/termux-packages](https://github.com/termux/termux-packages) | The build recipes for everything `pkg install` can reach. Also the answer to "why isn't X packaged" — usually because nobody has written the recipe yet. |
| [termux/proot-distro](https://github.com/termux/proot-distro) | The tool from this course's first half, plus the list of distributions beyond Debian and the plumbing behind `install`, `login` and `remove`. |
| [termux/termux-x11](https://github.com/termux/termux-x11) | The display-server project. Nightly builds and the issue tracker both live here, and it moves fast enough that the tracker is often more current than any write-up. |
| [r/termux](https://www.reddit.com/r/termux/) | Where "it works on every phone except mine" gets worked out by someone with the same phone. |

### Contributing a package is more approachable than it looks

This is the natural next step after the building lesson, and it's the one most
people assume is out of reach.

A package in `termux-packages` is a directory under `packages/` containing a
`build.sh` — homepage, description, version, source URL, and the SHA-256 of
that source. Many recipes are twenty lines. The infrastructure builds them in a
Docker image via the repo's own `scripts/run-docker.sh`, which means the actual
packaging work happens **on a computer, not on the phone** — the phone is where
you discovered the gap.

That's the honest shape of it: you already did the hard part when you got the
thing to compile by hand. Writing down what you did so the next person types
`pkg install` is the small part.

:::tip[Start by reading, not by writing]
Find a package whose upstream you understand, open its `build.sh`, and compare
it to what you did by hand. The conventions become obvious in about ten minutes,
and the repo's own docs cover the rest.
:::

## And when something breaks

It will, and this course breaks in more interesting ways than either of the
others — a good share of these failures are your specific device's opinion
rather than your typo.

- The [Command Cheatsheet](/reference/cheatsheet/) has every `proot-distro`,
  X11 and build invocation from this course on one page.
- [Troubleshooting](/reference/troubleshooting/) covers what this course
  actually produces: the install that dies partway through on a full disk, the
  black screen with three plausible causes, the container that can't see the
  display, and the renderer that quietly stayed on software.
- When neither helps, the issue tracker for the specific project — not the
  general forum — is where a device-specific bug gets an answer, because that's
  where the people who wrote it are.

## Recap

The specifics fade. These don't:

- **A phone is a real computer with real limits, and the limits are the
  kernel's, not yours.** No root means no root, all the way down. Everything
  PRoot cannot do traces back to that one fact.
- **Cost is a design input here.** Disk, battery, heat and time are all finite on
  a device you also need to make calls with. Check the space before the install,
  not after.
- **"It might not work on your device" is a real answer.** GPU acceleration is
  the clearest case, but it applies anywhere Android's fragmentation reaches.
  Knowing how to *check* beats trusting any instruction, including these.
- **Volume Down + letter is Ctrl.** It is the general one, and it's how you get
  your prompt back. Volume Up is a short fixed list, not an Alt key.
- **Code in `~`, backups in `~/storage/shared`.** Still true, still the way
  round people get wrong.
- **Close the doors you open.** Stop the tunnel, stop the server, remove the
  rootfs you're done with.
- **The wiki and the issue trackers outlive every tutorial.** Go to the source
  when something here stops matching what your phone does.

You started this series to find out whether a phone could be a Linux machine.
It can. You've now got the far more useful answer — which parts of that are
worth doing, and which parts are a laptop's job.

Go build something nobody packaged.
